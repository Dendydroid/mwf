46 What speech recognition hands you

The string in request_text has already been through a lossy process, and knowing its shape explains several things that otherwise look like model failures.

The stream, and its three kinds of result

A streaming recogniser emits interim hypotheses continuously and marks some results final. The distinction that matters is that a final segment is not necessarily the end of the turn: long utterances produce several finalised segments before the recogniser signals that speech is actually over.

SignalMeans
interim hypothesisBest guess so far. Will change. Useful for speculative work, never for a decision
final segmentThis span of audio is settled. More may follow in the same turn
end of speechThe turn is over. This is what produces a request to this service

What it does to the text

  • Punctuation is inferred, and often absent. A prompt that depends on sentence boundaries is depending on a guess.
  • Numbers arrive inconsistently — “twenty twenty-six”, “2026”, “20 26” for the same utterance. Parsers must accept words and digits for the same value, which is why a dedicated number parser exists rather than str::parse::<i64>.
  • Disfluencies survive. “uh”, “I mean — no, the”. This is the input the model is genuinely good at and a grammar never was.
  • Homophone errors are confident. “to” / “two”, “for” / “four”. The recogniser is not uncertain, it is wrong, so there is no confidence signal to catch it. This is precisely why read-back (Chapter 34) exists.
  • Names and codes are worst. Proper nouns and alphanumeric identifiers have no language model support. Anything in this class needs explicit confirmation, always.
Rule

Never treat the transcript as what the caller said. Treat it as the recogniser's best guess at what the caller said. Every design decision downstream — confirmation strategy, parser tolerance, the three-strike rule — follows from taking that seriously.

Eager end-of-turn

Some recognisers emit a speculative end-of-turn signal before the real one, so the language model can start prefilling. It costs 50–70% more model calls — some are cancelled — and saves 100–200 ms.

Worth knowing because it changes the traffic this service sees: requests that are cancelled mid-flight, and occasionally two requests for one turn. Both are things Chapter 28's idempotency handles, and neither is obvious until it appears in a metric as a mysterious inflation of turn count.

47 Turn detection, and the silence you pay for

Deciding when the caller has finished is the largest latency item in the whole pipeline, and none of it is compute.

Three approaches

MethodHowLatencyFails when
VAD only Classify each audio frame as speech or not; end the turn after N ms of silence Worst — you wait out the full threshold every turn The caller pauses mid-thought
STT endpointing Watch the transcript stream for completion signals rather than audio silence Middle. Does not need the full trailing pause Depends entirely on the recogniser's tuning
Model-based A small classifier reads partial transcripts and predicts whether the thought is finished Best — can fire before trailing silence False positives on incomplete sentences

STT endpointing is the recommended default for most production agents. Model-based detection is the low-latency option — LiveKit ships a ~135M-parameter fine-tune that runs locally and predicts whether the current transcript looks like a finished thought, combined with acoustic VAD for pacing.

The number that matters

An 800 ms silence timeout adds nearly a full second to every single response before the pipeline starts. Against Chapter 3's 1,115 ms total budget, that one setting can consume most of it.

The thresholds are tuned per vertical, and the published starting points are worth knowing even when someone else owns the dial:

ContextSilence before end-of-turn
IVR replacement — short, known answers~250 ms
Sales — conversational, longer turns~400 ms
Healthcare intake — considered answers, thinking pauses~600 ms

This is the most valuable question to ask whoever operates the phone platform, because it is the largest number in the budget and it is a configuration value rather than an engineering project. The answer also tells you which threshold this service's own latency is competing against: a 250 ms endpoint leaves far less headroom for a 2.8-second turn than a 600 ms one does.

Trap

Turn detection interacts with the design in Chapter 32 in a way that is easy to miss. A question that invites a considered answer — “what date would suit you?” — produces thinking pauses, which a short threshold cuts in half, which produces a no-match, which produces a re-prompt. The fix is sometimes in the prompt: a more closed question produces a shorter, more fluent answer. A no-match problem is not always a recognition problem.

48 Barge-in, and what the caller actually heard

Barge-in is the caller speaking while the agent is talking. Handling it means keeping turn detection active during playback, and cancelling synthesis the moment caller speech is detected.

Turn detection decides when speech started or ended; interruption handling decides what to do about it while the agent is mid-sentence. They are separate problems and are often conflated.

The part that reaches this service

The agent said: “I have you down for the second of October at three o'clock, for four people — shall I confirm that?”

The caller interrupted after “second of October at”.

The conversation history must now record what was heard, not what was generated. If it records the full sentence, the system believes it read back a time and a party size that the caller never heard, and will treat a subsequent “yes” as confirming all of it.

Platforms expose this: on interruption, a synthesis API reports how much audio played, the text actually spoken, and the text left unspoken.

Rule

A value is confirmed only if the caller heard it read back. When the platform reports a barge-in, the turn window records the spoken prefix, and any slot whose read-back fell in the unspoken remainder stays unconfirmed. A confirmation flag set from a sentence nobody finished hearing is a false record, and it is exactly the kind that surfaces as a disputed booking.

Whether this information reaches this service is a platform question, and it is worth asking early. If it does not, the conservative fallback is to treat any barge-in as invalidating every confirmation in that turn — which costs a turn and is always safe.

Backchannels are not interruptions

“Mm-hm”, “yeah”, “right” spoken over the agent are acknowledgements, not attempts to take the floor. Treating them as barge-in produces an agent that stops every time the caller signals they are listening — which trains the caller into silence, and silence is what Chapter 47's thresholds are waiting for.

49 Synthesis, telephony, and owning this one day

Synthesis

The smallest slice of the budget — 100 ms in Twilio's allocation, and streaming systems have reported first audio as low as 80 ms through interleaved text-audio generation, which means response length does not increase initial latency.

The two things that matter from here: streaming synthesis, which starts producing audio from partial text, and the chunking rule between the model's token stream and it — sentence boundaries, excluding abbreviations and decimals, with a minimum chunk length so fragments are not sent. That is the mechanism Chapter 27's schema reorder feeds.

Operational details that bite: streaming sessions expire (two hours in one documented case), need periodic keep-alives, and reconnection resets prosody state mid-call.

DTMF deserves more attention than it usually gets. It has a 100% recognition rate, it works for callers in noisy places and for accents the recogniser handles badly, and offering it after two failures is often better than any amount of prompt engineering.

If this layer ever becomes yours

Two open frameworks dominate, and they have converged on the same shape. Pipecat models an agent as a pipeline of processors through which typed frames flow — audio chunks, transcript fragments, tokens, interruption signals — with each processor handling the frame types it understands and passing the rest through. LiveKit Agents builds on a WebRTC transport with turn detection, interruption handling and a large plugin set.

Both give you turn detection, barge-in and the streaming plumbing, and both leave everything in Parts II–VIII entirely to you. That is the useful observation: adopting one of them does not change the dialogue layer, the prompt layer, the serving layer or the compliance obligations. It replaces the boundary, not the system.

Rule

Do not build this layer. The components that make a cascade work — low-latency recognition, expressive synthesis, semantic turn detection — are where the specialist providers differentiate, and none of them is where this project's value is. Keep the boundary at text in, sentence out, and keep it clean enough that the thing on the other side can be replaced.
